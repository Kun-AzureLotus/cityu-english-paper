# Academic editing decisions

## By section

- Abstract: retain research question, actual design/data, actual findings, and the stated contribution. Cut generic opening claims when they add nothing; do not invent results to make an abstract complete.
- Introduction: clarify the existing problem and scope. A novelty claim such as “the first study” requires evidence; replacing it with “no prior study” strengthens the same unsupported claim.
- Literature review: preserve attribution and disagreements. Help distinguish findings from interpretations; do not merge different authors' positions or fabricate consensus. Suggest synthesis questions when the student's analysis is missing.
- Methods: preserve reproducibility, exact procedures, settings, and sample details. Passive voice and repeated technical terms may be useful. Do not invent a rationale or replace a method with a more impressive one.
- Results: preserve numbers and test names. Do not infer statistical significance, causation, generalizability, or an effect size absent from supplied results. Missing uncertainty estimates are an author query.
- Discussion: distinguish observed findings, plausible interpretation, and speculation. Preserve limits and disciplinary hedging. Do not add a fashionable implication unsupported by the analysis.
- Conclusion: retain the answer and necessary boundaries. Remove repeated promotional closure; keep a recap when the assignment expects one.

## Pattern diagnosis

“In today's rapidly evolving landscape” can often go when the next sentence supplies the actual context. “Furthermore” can stay when it connects genuinely additional evidence. “This study demonstrates” is not a forbidden phrase; check whether the evidence supports that certainty.

Nominalizations are an editing opportunity, not a ban. “An evaluation of model performance was conducted” can become “The study evaluated model performance” only if that subject is correct. Preserve established noun phrases used as technical terms.

Repeated paragraph scaffolds deserve inspection, not automatic destruction. A structured comparison may intentionally repeat headings. Citation-rich review paragraphs can remain formal and consistent without being labelled AI-written.

## Source-faithful examples (invented teaching inputs)

Input: “It is important to note that the survey included 84 respondents from two firms. These findings may suggest an association between training and reported confidence.”
Revision: “The survey included 84 respondents from two firms. The findings suggest a possible association between training and reported confidence.”
Check: retains 84, two firms, self-reported confidence, and association rather than causation. The phrase “may suggest” is simplified without removing uncertainty.

Input: “The intervention did not significantly improve accuracy (p = .18).”
Revision: “The intervention did not produce a statistically significant improvement in accuracy (p = .18).”
Do not write “The intervention had no effect” or add an effect size.

Input: “The approach is efficient, compact and accurate.”
Possible edit: “The approach is efficient, compact, and accurate.”
Author query if support is required: “What results support each performance claim?”
Do not add “compared with existing methods” or claim compactness caused accuracy.

Input: “Previous studies have conclusively established the superiority of the method (Lee, 2023).”
Action: retain the citation and flag the strength of “conclusively” and “superiority” for source checking. Without the source, do not assert Lee proved a result, invent a limitation, or silently change the author's conclusion.

Input: a well-supported three-item comparison using passive voice in Methods.
Action: preserve it if clear. No edit is a valid outcome.

## Final semantic review

Check the research question, thesis, main claims, participant/sample scope, measured variables, comparison baselines, direction of effects, numbers/units, dates, citations/quotes, causal language, negations, qualifications, limitations, and tense. Keep author queries outside submission-ready text unless the user requested annotated output.
